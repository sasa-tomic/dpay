02 The handoff

One of you needs a signal. It isn't you.

Three moves, and only the shop ever touches the network. You stay dark the whole time.

1

The shop rings it up

A code on their screen or a tap on their reader, for the exact amount. $12, to that café. Nothing for you to type, nothing to convert.

2

You tap — offline

Your phone signs it on the spot, no connection involved. The signature locks in that amount and that shop, so it can't be replayed or nudged higher.

3

Cleared in a second or two

The shop — the side with wifi — sends your signed payment on and watches it settle. Dollars land before you've pocketed your phone.

03 The edge

Built for the person holding a dead phone.

Every contactless payment you've made assumed you were online at the register. Abroad, that's the assumption that breaks first.

You're the offline one — by design.

Cards and phone wallets put the payer online at the moment of tap. dpay flips the roles: the online side is the shop with wifi, and you get to be the one without a signal — because abroad, you always are.

No foreign-card FX toll

You send dollars; the shop is paid the same dollars. Nothing routes through a card network to skim a cross-border spread. Same in, same out.

The key never leaves your phone

There's no company account in the middle. dpay can't move, hold, or freeze your money — not by policy, but because the design gives it no way to.

Straight talk

“You don't need signal” isn't “nobody does.” If the shop is offline too — a stall up a mountain — your signed payment waits with them and settles the moment they reconnect. You still get told when it clears.
